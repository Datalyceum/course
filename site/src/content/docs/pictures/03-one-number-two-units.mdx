## The story

NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter launched in December 1998. After about nine months in space it reached Mars in September 1999, and was lost as it went into orbit.

On the way, small thruster firings nudged its path, and the team on the ground tracked every one. One piece of ground software reported the size of those firings in **pound-force seconds** (lbf·s). The navigation software read the same numbers as **newton-seconds** (N·s), the unit the contract asked for.

One pound-force second is about 4.45 newton-seconds. So every firing looked about 4.45 times weaker than it really was, the small errors added up over the months, and the orbiter arrived far too low. It dipped into the Martian atmosphere and was lost.

## The real failure

The investigation traced the loss to the unit mix-up. But NASA's message afterwards was that the error itself wasn't the real failure. People make mistakes. The failure was that none of the checks between the teams caught it.

## What it means for data

Every dataset is a handoff. Someone measured, someone typed, someone exported, and now you're reading it. A number without a unit is a guess about what the sender meant.

- **Label your units** in column names or in a data dictionary: `body_mass_g`, not `mass`.
- **Check units at every handoff,** especially when data moves between teams, tools or countries.
- **Look at the range.** A column of penguin weights between 2.7 and 6.3 is probably kilograms; between 2,700 and 6,300, grams. A column with both is a problem.
