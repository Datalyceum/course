## The story

Every three years the Federal Reserve asks thousands of American families about their finances: what they own, what they owe, what they earn. The results of the 2025 survey came out in October 2026.

The average net worth of a family was **$1.24 million**. Read that way, the typical American family is a millionaire. But line the families up from least to most and look at the one in the middle, and that family is worth **$215,900**. More than five times less.

Neither number is wrong. They are two different kinds of average.

## Two kinds of average

- **The mean** adds up every value and divides by how many there are. It's what most people mean by "the average".
- **The median** sorts the values and takes the one in the middle. Half are above it, half below.

When the values are spread evenly around the middle, the two land close together. When a few values are huge, they split. The mean has to share those huge values across everyone, so it rises. The median only cares about which value sits in the middle, so it hardly moves.

Wealth is a clear case. A small number of families hold enormous fortunes, and they pull the mean far above what most families have. That's why most families are worth much less than the average.

## What it means for data

- **Look at the shape first.** If a few values are much bigger than the rest, as with wealth, income, house prices or the time customers wait, expect the mean to sit above the median.
- **Report the median for the typical case,** and the mean when the total matters, such as the money a whole country holds or a whole team spends.
- **Better, report both, and say which one you mean.** "Average" on its own can hide a gap like this one.

The same thing happens inside machine learning. A model that is scored by its average error can look fine while a few cases are badly wrong, so data people check the middle and the spread too.
