## The story

In 1959, Arthur Samuel, an engineer at IBM, published a paper on a program that played checkers. His claim was bold for the time: a computer could learn to play a better game than the person who wrote the program. The paper also helped give the field its name: machine learning.

## Clues, not moves

Samuel never typed in good moves. He gave the program a way to score a position, built from clues he chose:

- **Pieces ahead:** how many more pieces it has than the opponent.
- **Kings:** pieces that reached the far side and can move both ways.
- **Control of the centre:** pieces on the middle squares.

Each clue gets a number, its **weight**, that says how much it counts. Add up every clue times its weight, and you get the program's guess of how good a position is. The program didn't know the right weights. That's what it had to learn.

## The loop

1. **Guess.** Start with weights that are only a guess, and score the position.
2. **Check.** Look a few moves further ahead and score the position again. If the two scores disagree, the first one was wrong.
3. **Adjust.** Nudge the weights a little, so the quick score gets closer to what the deeper look found.
4. **Repeat.** Play again, often against a copy of itself, and keep nudging.

Over many games the weights settle, and the program plays better than when it started.

## Why it matters today

The same loop trains today's models. A model makes a prediction, training measures how far off it was, and the weights get nudged to make the next prediction a little better. A modern network has millions or billions of weights instead of a few dozen, and the nudging is done with maths called gradient descent, but the idea hasn't changed.

## Things to keep in mind

- **The clues came from a person.** Samuel chose what the program should look at. Picking good inputs, called features, is still a big part of the work. Deep learning goes one step further and learns its own features from raw data.
- **It never understood checkers.** It learned which numbers lead to good positions, nothing more.
- **The bars are an illustration.** They show the idea of weights changing, not Samuel's real numbers.
- **Not every model learns by nudging weights.** The decision tree in [Lesson 0.3](/season-0/lesson-0-3-how-machines-learn/) learns by trying splits and keeping the best one. The spirit is the same: try, check, keep what works.
